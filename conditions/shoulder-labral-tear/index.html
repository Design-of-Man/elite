Shoulder Labral Tear and Instability

A shoulder that catches, clicks or feels like it could slip out may have a torn labrum, a common injury in throwing athletes and after dislocations.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: SLAP tear, Bankart lesion, torn shoulder labrum, shoulder instability, recurrent shoulder dislocation

About Shoulder Labral Tear and Instability

The labrum is a firm rim of cartilage around the edge of your shoulder socket (the glenoid). According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, it deepens the socket by up to 50 percent and gives the stabilizing ligaments, along with one end of the biceps tendon, a place to attach. When the labrum tears, the ball of the upper arm bone sits less securely and may slip partway or fully out of the socket.

Tears are named by location. A SLAP tear involves the top of the labrum where the biceps attaches and is common in throwing athletes. A Bankart lesion sits at the front and bottom of the socket, usually after the ball dislocates forward. Posterior tears affect the back. Mayo Clinic notes that dislocations are most common in teens and people in their 20s, and that a prior dislocation raises the chance of another.

What are the symptoms?

  • A sense that the shoulder is loose or might slip out
  • Catching, locking, popping or grinding in the joint
  • Deep, aching pain with overhead activity or throwing
  • Pain at night
  • Reduced strength and range of motion
  • Repeated episodes of the shoulder giving out or dislocating

What causes it?

  • Falling on an outstretched arm
  • A direct blow to the shoulder or a sudden pull on the arm
  • A previous shoulder dislocation
  • Repetitive overhead motion in throwing sports and weightlifting
  • Contact and collision sports such as football and hockey
  • Naturally loose ligaments (hyperlaxity)

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

Not every labral tear needs an operation. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists physical therapy as first-line care, with bracing for some unstable shoulders, and its instability guidance centers on physical therapy that strengthens the shoulder muscles and improves shoulder control. Surgery is considered when pain, catching or repeated dislocations continue. Dr. Matarazzo weighs your age, your sport and how many times the shoulder has come out when helping you choose between the two.

Physical therapy for stability

A guided program strengthens the rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles so they keep the ball centered in the socket, backed by exercises you continue at home.

Activity changes and anti-inflammatories

Pausing throwing, overhead lifting and contact play while pain settles, with NSAIDs if they are safe for you, reduces repeated stress on the torn rim.

Bracing

For some instability, AAOS notes a brace can help by limiting the arm positions that let the shoulder slip.

Arthroscopic labral repair

Through small incisions, the torn labrum is reattached to the socket rim with suture anchors. This is typically a same-day outpatient procedure.

Open stabilization

Some instability cases are better suited to an open procedure through a shoulder incision, which lets the surgeon see and repair the structures directly.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo asks how the problem began, whether the shoulder has ever come out of place and how often, and what sports or work you do. The exam checks how loose the joint is, which arm positions bring on pain or a feeling of slipping, and your strength and motion. X-rays look for bone injury. An MRI, sometimes with contrast dye (an arthrogram), shows the labrum best, and a CT scan can measure bone loss after repeat dislocations.

What does recovery look like?

With therapy alone, results depend on how well stronger muscles control the joint, and Mayo Clinic notes a shoulder that has dislocated carries a higher risk of doing so again. After arthroscopic labral repair, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says to expect a sling for 2 to 6 weeks and limits on lifting for about 12 weeks, with the shoulder fully healed in 4 to 6 months. When you return to throwing or contact sport is decided with your surgeon and therapist based on strength, motion and the demands of your sport.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Avoid the arm positions that make the shoulder feel unstable, often reaching overhead or out to the side and back.
  • Hold off on throwing, overhead lifting and contact sports until you have been evaluated.
  • Use a cold pack for up to 20 minutes at a time to ease soreness after activity.
  • Keep moving the shoulder gently in comfortable ranges so it does not stiffen.

When should I get care right away?

  • A shoulder that looks out of place or misshapen after a fall or hit: go to an emergency room or urgent care, and do not try to pop it back in
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the arm after an injury
  • An arm that feels unusually hot or cold, or is badly swollen
  • Repeated episodes of the shoulder giving out
  • Catching or locking that keeps returning and limits your activity
