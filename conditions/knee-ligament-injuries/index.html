MCL, PCL and LCL Knee Ligament Injuries

The ligaments on the sides and back of your knee can stretch or tear with a hit, twist or fall. Many heal with bracing and rehab, and some need reconstruction.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: knee sprain, MCL tear, LCL tear, PCL tear, collateral ligament injury

What are MCL, PCL and LCL injuries?

Four main ligaments hold the knee together. The medial collateral ligament (MCL) runs along the inner side from the thighbone to the shinbone, and the lateral collateral ligament (LCL) runs along the outer side from the thighbone to the smaller fibula. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) explains that these two control side-to-side motion. The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) sits inside the joint behind the ACL and keeps the shinbone from sliding backward.

Each injury tends to have its own story. MCL tears often come from a blow to the outside of the knee or a hard cut in football, soccer or skiing. Cleveland Clinic notes that an LCL tear is rarely isolated and often comes with an ACL tear or another knee injury. The PCL is stronger than the ACL, and the AAOS says it is rarely hurt by a simple misstep. A typical cause is the bent knee striking a car dashboard.

What are the symptoms?

  • Pain on the inner or outer side of the knee
  • Swelling and tenderness over the injured ligament
  • A pop at the moment of injury
  • A wobbly or unstable feeling, as if the knee may give out
  • Stiffness and trouble walking
  • With a PCL tear, the knee may look like it sags backward when bent
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the lower leg, which needs prompt attention

What causes it?

  • A blow to the outside of the knee (MCL) or inner side of the knee (LCL)
  • A bent knee striking a car dashboard (PCL)
  • Falling onto a bent knee during sports
  • Sudden twisting, cutting or hyperextension, as in skiing
  • Contact sports such as football and soccer

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

According to the AAOS, low-grade MCL and LCL injuries without other damage, and many isolated PCL tears, are treated without surgery. Rest, ice, anti-inflammatory medicine, a brace and physical therapy let the ligament heal while you regain motion and strength. Surgery enters the picture when a ligament is torn off the bone, when several ligaments are injured together, or when the knee stays unstable after rehab. Dr. Matarazzo grades each ligament on exam and imaging, because the grade and any combined injury drive the plan.

Hinged knee brace

A hinged brace protects a healing MCL or LCL from side-to-side stress while still letting the knee bend, and a PCL brace helps keep the shinbone from sagging backward.

Early swelling control

Rest, ice, compression, elevation and crutches as needed help in the first days, especially for moderate and severe sprains.

Physical therapy

Rehab restores range of motion first, then strength. For PCL injuries the AAOS emphasizes quadriceps strengthening, since those muscles help hold the shinbone forward.

Ligament repair or reconstruction

When a ligament is pulled off the bone, fully torn with instability, or injured along with the ACL or meniscus, it may be reattached or rebuilt with a tissue graft, often arthroscopically.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo will ask how the knee was hit or twisted, then press along each ligament to find the sore spot. He uses stress tests that push the knee gently inward, outward and backward, including the posterior drawer test for the PCL, comparing against your other knee. He also checks the nerves and circulation of the lower leg. X-rays look for small fractures where a ligament pulled off bone, and an MRI shows the full extent of damage.

What does recovery look like?

Healing time follows the grade of the sprain. Cleveland Clinic lists typical MCL recovery as about 1 to 3 weeks for grade 1, 4 to 6 weeks for grade 2, and 6 weeks or more for grade 3. For LCL injuries, Cleveland Clinic gives about 3 to 4 weeks for grade 1 and 8 to 12 weeks for grades 2 and 3. After PCL reconstruction, the AAOS reports physical therapy usually starts 1 to 4 weeks after surgery, with full recovery taking 6 to 12 months. Combined injuries take longer.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Rest the knee and use crutches if it is painful to walk.
  • Ice for 10 to 20 minutes at a time in the first days to limit swelling.
  • Wrap the knee with a compression bandage and keep it elevated when you can.
  • Avoid sports, cutting and twisting until the knee has been examined.

When should I get care right away?

  • Go to the emergency room right away if the knee looked dislocated or out of place after an injury, even if it slipped back, or if your foot or leg turns cold, pale, numb or weak. A knee dislocation can damage the main artery and nerves behind the knee.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the lower leg, foot or toes
  • You cannot put weight on the leg
  • Rapid, heavy swelling after an injury
  • The knee gives way when you walk
  • Pain, swelling or a bluish color in the calf

MCL, PCL and LCL Knee Ligament Injuries: Common Questions

Many people can walk with a mild or moderate MCL sprain, though it may be painful and the inner knee is often tender. With a grade 3 tear the knee can feel loose and unstable, and crutches with a hinged brace are commonly used. Walking comfort is a poor guide to severity, so it is worth having the grade checked by exam.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons grades ligament injuries in three levels. Grade 1 means the ligament is stretched but still intact. Grade 2 is a partial tear with some looseness. Grade 3 is a complete tear and the knee is unstable. The grade, together with any other damaged structures, guides bracing, rehab length and whether surgery is worth discussing.

Many isolated PCL tears are treated without surgery. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes initial rest, ice and compression, a brace that keeps the shinbone from sagging backward, and physical therapy focused on quadriceps strength. Reconstruction is more often considered when the PCL is injured along with other ligaments or when the knee remains unstable after rehab.

The MCL runs along the inner side of the knee and resists the knee buckling inward, while the ACL sits in the center of the joint and stops the shinbone from sliding forward. Many MCL tears heal with a brace and rehab. ACL tears more often leave the knee feeling unstable when you pivot. The two can tear together, which is why both are checked after a knee injury.

Cleveland Clinic notes that an LCL tear is rarely an isolated injury. The force that damages the outer knee often injures the ACL or other knee structures as well. Numbness, tingling or weakness in the lower leg after this kind of injury also needs prompt evaluation. An MRI helps map every damaged structure so treatment addresses the whole knee.

A hinged knee brace is a common part of treatment for collateral ligament sprains, according to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, because it protects against side-to-side stress while the knee can still bend. The right brace and how long to wear it depend on the ligament and the grade, so get the injury examined before choosing one off the shelf.
