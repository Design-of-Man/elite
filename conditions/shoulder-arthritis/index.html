Shoulder Arthritis

When a stiff, grinding shoulder starts limiting how you dress, reach or sleep, arthritis may be the reason, and care ranges from exercise to joint replacement.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: glenohumeral arthritis, shoulder osteoarthritis, AC joint arthritis, rotator cuff tear arthropathy

What is shoulder arthritis?

Your shoulder has two joints that arthritis can affect. The main one is the glenohumeral joint, where the ball at the top of your upper arm bone fits into the socket of your shoulder blade. The second is the acromioclavicular (AC) joint, where the collarbone meets the top of the shoulder blade. In arthritis, the smooth cartilage covering these surfaces wears away, and the bones start to rub against each other.

According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, osteoarthritis (wear-and-tear arthritis) is more common in people over 50. Rheumatoid arthritis, an autoimmune disease, usually affects both shoulders. Arthritis can also follow a fracture or dislocation, develop after a large rotator cuff tear has been present for years, or result from avascular necrosis, when blood flow to the ball of the humerus is disrupted.

What are the symptoms?

  • Deep shoulder pain that gets worse with activity
  • Pain that builds gradually over months or years
  • Stiffness and lost motion, such as trouble reaching a high shelf
  • Grinding, clicking or cracking (crepitus) when you move the arm
  • Pain at rest that can interfere with sleep
  • Difficulty with everyday tasks like dressing or washing as it advances

What causes it?

  • Age-related wear of joint cartilage, more common after 50
  • Rheumatoid arthritis or another inflammatory joint disease
  • A past fracture or shoulder dislocation (posttraumatic arthritis)
  • A large rotator cuff tear left in place for a long time (cuff tear arthropathy)
  • Loss of blood supply to the humeral head (avascular necrosis)

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

Treatment for shoulder arthritis aims to ease pain and keep you using the arm for the things that matter to you. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons and Johns Hopkins Medicine both describe a stepwise approach: exercise, activity changes, medicine and injections first, then surgery when pain still interferes with daily life. Dr. Matarazzo matches the step to how much the joint has changed on X-ray, the condition of your rotator cuff, and how much the shoulder is limiting you.

Range of motion exercise and physical therapy

Gentle stretching and strengthening help preserve the motion you still have and keep the surrounding muscles working, which can make daily tasks less painful.

Activity changes, ice and heat

Adjusting how you lift and reach, and using ice or heat around activity, can reduce the number and intensity of flare-ups.

Medication

Acetaminophen or anti-inflammatory drugs can relieve pain. People with rheumatoid arthritis may also need disease-modifying drugs from a rheumatologist.

Injections

A corticosteroid injection can calm a painful flare, and Johns Hopkins also lists hyaluronic acid injections. Some patients ask about orthobiologics, an option whose evidence is still developing and which does not rebuild cartilage.

Total or reverse total shoulder replacement

When pain at rest or with daily tasks persists despite other care, the worn surfaces can be replaced. AAOS notes the reverse design is used when the rotator cuff is also badly damaged.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo asks where the pain sits, how long it has been building, what makes it worse, and whether you have had a shoulder injury, a rotator cuff problem or an inflammatory condition. He measures your range of motion, checks for tenderness and grinding, and tests rotator cuff strength, since a weak cuff changes the treatment options. X-rays show joint space narrowing and bone spurs, and a CT scan may be ordered if more detail is needed.

What does recovery look like?

Nonsurgical care aims for steadier days and fewer flare-ups rather than a cure, and progress varies from person to person. If you have a shoulder replacement, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says many patients go home the day after surgery and wear a sling for the first 2 to 6 weeks. Plan on no driving, and no lifting anything heavier than a glass of water, for 2 to 6 weeks. AAOS adds that most people can handle simple tasks such as eating, dressing and grooming within 2 weeks, with strength and motion improving through a home exercise program.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Keep the shoulder moving with gentle range of motion exercises instead of holding it still.
  • Use a warm pack before activity and a cold pack afterward, for up to 20 minutes at a time.
  • Change how you do tasks that aggravate pain, such as using a step stool instead of reaching for a high shelf.
  • Take acetaminophen or an anti-inflammatory as directed, if your doctor says they are safe for you.

When should I get care right away?

  • Pain that keeps you awake at night or stops you from dressing, washing or toileting
  • A shoulder that suddenly changes shape or becomes badly swollen
  • Severe pain in both shoulders, or shoulder pain with fever or redness
  • Shoulder pain that keeps getting worse or does not improve after 2 weeks of self-care
  • Call 911 for sudden pressure or crushing pain in the shoulder, especially if it runs from the chest to the left jaw, arm or neck, or comes with shortness of breath, dizziness or sweating

Shoulder Arthritis: Common Questions

Bone on bone is a common way of describing advanced arthritis, where the cartilage cushioning the ball and socket has worn so thin that little space shows between the bones on X-ray. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that X-rays in shoulder arthritis often show this narrowing along with bone spurs. Many people with advanced changes still try nonsurgical care first, and replacement becomes a consideration when pain limits daily life.

Yes, for many people. Johns Hopkins Medicine and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons list range of motion exercises, physical therapy, activity changes, ice or heat, pain and anti-inflammatory medicines, and injections as first steps. These treatments do not reverse arthritis, but they can lower pain and help you keep using the arm. Surgery is usually considered when these measures no longer control your symptoms.

In a total shoulder replacement, a metal ball replaces the top of the arm bone and a plastic surface lines the socket, mimicking normal anatomy. A reverse total shoulder replacement switches their positions, placing the ball on the shoulder blade and the socket on the arm bone. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, the reverse design is used for shoulders with significant rotator cuff damage, such as cuff tear arthropathy.

Corticosteroid injections are a standard option the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists for shoulder arthritis, and they can calm pain and inflammation during a flare. Relief is temporary and varies between people. Your surgeon will talk with you about how often injections make sense and whether hyaluronic acid, which Johns Hopkins Medicine also lists as an option, is worth considering for your shoulder.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes typical candidates as people with severe pain that interferes with tasks like reaching into a cabinet, dressing, toileting or washing, moderate to severe pain at rest that may disturb sleep, and little improvement after medicines, injections and physical therapy. The decision comes down to how much the shoulder limits you, your X-ray findings and your overall health.

Both the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons and Johns Hopkins Medicine describe rotator cuff tear arthropathy, a type of shoulder arthritis that develops after a large, long-standing rotator cuff tear. Because the cuff itself is damaged, this form of arthritis is often treated differently from ordinary osteoarthritis, and a reverse shoulder replacement is one of the options a surgeon may discuss.
