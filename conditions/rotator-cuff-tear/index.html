Rotator Cuff Tear

Shoulder pain that wakes you at night, or weakness when you lift your arm, can point to a rotator cuff tear, and most tears have several treatment paths.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: torn rotator cuff, rotator cuff rupture, partial-thickness rotator cuff tear, full-thickness rotator cuff tear

What is a rotator cuff tear?

Your rotator cuff is a group of four muscles whose tendons wrap around the ball of your upper arm bone (the humerus) and keep it centered in the shallow shoulder socket. A tear happens when one or more of those tendons frays or pulls away from the bone. A partial tear leaves the tendon attached but thinned. A full-thickness tear goes all the way through, and in some cases the tendon detaches completely.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) reports that almost 2 million people in the United States visit their doctors each year because of rotator cuff tears. Risk climbs after age 40, and the dominant arm is affected more often. Tennis players, baseball pitchers, painters and carpenters put extra strain on the tendons. Some tears follow a fall, while many develop slowly as the tendon wears and its blood supply declines with age.

What are the symptoms?

  • Pain at rest and at night, especially lying on the affected shoulder
  • Pain when you lift or lower your arm, or reach overhead or behind your back
  • Weakness when you raise or rotate the arm
  • A crackling feeling (crepitus) as the shoulder moves
  • Sudden intense pain and immediate weakness after a fall or heavy lift
  • Stiffness and loss of motion that slowly worsen

What causes it?

  • Falling on an outstretched arm
  • Lifting something heavy with a jerking motion
  • Repetitive overhead work or sport, such as tennis, pitching, painting and carpentry
  • Age over 40, as blood supply to the cuff tendons decreases
  • Long-standing tendinitis or impingement that gradually wears the tendon down

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

Many rotator cuff tears can be managed without an operation. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, nonsurgical treatment relieves pain and improves function in about 80 to 85 percent of patients. Dr. Matarazzo generally starts there unless a recent injury caused the tear or the shoulder has lost significant strength. Because AAOS notes that a tear can get larger over time, he also rechecks strength and symptoms to judge whether a repair would serve you better than continued conservative care.

Activity changes and rest

Limiting overhead reaching and the motions that provoke pain lets an irritated tendon calm down, while gentle movement keeps the shoulder from stiffening.

Physical therapy

Stretching restores motion first, then strengthening builds up the intact rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles so they can share the load the torn tendon used to carry.

Anti-inflammatory medicine

Over-the-counter ibuprofen or naproxen can ease pain and swelling when they are safe for you.

Steroid injection

AAOS reports that a cortisone injection relieves pain in about two-thirds of patients for at least 3 months, which can make it easier to complete therapy.

Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair

When pain lasts 6 to 12 months, the tear is larger than 3 cm with good tissue, or an acute injury caused it, the tendon can be reattached to bone through small incisions, usually as same-day surgery.

Reverse shoulder replacement for tear arthropathy

If a large, long-standing tear has led to arthritis (rotator cuff tear arthropathy), AAOS describes a reverse total shoulder replacement as an option.

What happens at the first visit?

Expect questions about when the pain started, whether a fall or lift set it off, how you sleep, and what you have already tried. Dr. Matarazzo checks for tenderness, measures range of motion, and tests the strength of each rotator cuff muscle in different arm positions. He may examine your neck to rule out a pinched nerve. X-rays look at the bones and for arthritis, and an MRI or ultrasound can show the location and size of a tear.

What does recovery look like?

How a tear responds depends on its size, how long it has been present, your age and how active you were beforehand, according to MedlinePlus. Nonsurgical care asks for steady exercise that your therapist advances as pain allows. After an arthroscopic repair, AAOS says most people wear a sling and avoid using the arm for the first 4 to 6 weeks, begin therapist-guided motion in that window, and start strengthening around 8 to 12 weeks. Most patients reach functional motion and adequate strength by 4 to 6 months after surgery, and some take longer.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Avoid overhead reaching and heavy lifting with the sore arm, but keep it moving gently within a comfortable range.
  • Ice the shoulder for 15 minutes, then leave it off for 15 minutes, 3 to 4 times a day for the first 2 to 3 days, with the ice wrapped in cloth.
  • Over-the-counter ibuprofen or naproxen can help if your doctor has said they are safe for you.
  • Sit and stand with your shoulders down and gently back during desk work.

When should I get care right away?

  • Sudden severe pain, or being unable to raise your arm, after a fall or injury
  • Shoulder pain together with fever, redness or swelling
  • Pain that lasts more than 2 to 4 weeks even after home treatment
  • Pins and needles or numbness in the arm that do not go away
  • Call 911 for sudden pressure or crushing pain in the shoulder, especially if it runs from the chest to the left jaw, arm or neck, or comes with shortness of breath, dizziness or sweating

Rotator Cuff Tear: Common Questions

A full-thickness tear that has pulled back from the bone will not reattach by itself, according to MedlinePlus. That does not mean every tear needs surgery. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons reports that about 80 to 85 percent of patients get pain relief and better function with nonsurgical care such as physical therapy, activity changes and medicine, even though the tear itself remains.

Tendinitis and tears both cause pain with overhead motion and at night, so symptoms alone rarely settle the question. True weakness when you lift or rotate the arm, or sudden weakness after a fall, raises suspicion of a tear. An orthopedic surgeon tests strength in specific positions and may order an MRI or ultrasound, which can show whether the tendon is torn and how large the tear is.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists several reasons to consider repair: symptoms that have lasted 6 to 12 months, a tear larger than 3 cm with good surrounding tissue, significant weakness and loss of shoulder function, or a tear caused by a recent acute injury. AAOS notes that continued pain is the main reason people choose surgery. A surgical opinion helps you weigh these factors against your goals.

Arthroscopic repair uses a small camera and tiny incisions and is usually a same-day procedure. A mini-open repair uses a somewhat larger incision, and MedlinePlus notes that large tears may need open surgery. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, patients rate these methods about the same for pain relief, strength improvement and overall satisfaction. Your surgeon recommends an approach based on the size and pattern of your tear.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that a rotator cuff tear can get larger over time, and MedlinePlus describes chronic tears whose pain, weakness and stiffness worsen gradually. A large tear left for years can lead to rotator cuff tear arthropathy, a form of shoulder arthritis. Rechecking your strength over time helps you and your surgeon decide whether watching the tear still makes sense.
