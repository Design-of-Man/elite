Meniscus Tear

A torn meniscus can make your knee catch, swell or ache when you twist. Many tears settle with rest and rehab, and some are best repaired.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: torn meniscus, torn cartilage in the knee, bucket handle tear, degenerative meniscus tear

What is a meniscus tear?

Each knee has two menisci, wedge-shaped pads of tough fibrocartilage that sit between the thighbone and the shinbone. One lies on the inner side of the knee and one on the outer side. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) describes them as shock absorbers that help pass your body weight through the joint and keep it steady. Tears are named by their shape, such as flap, radial or bucket handle tears.

Two groups tend to tear a meniscus. Younger, active people often do it with a forceful pivot or cut during contact sports, sometimes alongside an ACL injury. Older adults are more likely to have a degenerative tear, where tissue weakened by age gives way with very little force. The AAOS notes that an awkward twist while getting up from a chair can be enough. Meniscus tears happen at every age, so a sports history is not required.

What are the symptoms?

  • A pop felt at the moment of injury
  • Pain along the inner or outer side of the knee
  • Swelling and stiffness
  • Catching or locking, as if something is stuck in the joint
  • A feeling that the knee may give way
  • Trouble fully straightening or bending the leg

What causes it?

  • Pivoting or cutting with the foot planted
  • Contact sports and sports with sudden direction changes
  • Age-related wear that weakens the meniscus
  • An awkward twist during an everyday movement
  • A coexisting ACL tear or knee arthritis

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

The AAOS and Mayo Clinic both describe nonsurgical care as a sensible starting point for many tears. Rest, ice, anti-inflammatory medicine and a strengthening program often calm symptoms, and Mayo Clinic notes that many tears that do not lock the knee become less painful over time. Dr. Matarazzo looks at the tear's type and location, your age and activity level, and whether the knee mechanically locks. When surgery makes sense, repair is favored for tissue with a chance to heal, and trimming is reserved for pieces that cannot.

Relative rest and activity changes

Avoiding twisting and pivoting for a while, sometimes with crutches, gives an irritated knee time to settle.

Strengthening program

Physical therapy builds the thigh and hip muscles that support the knee, which Mayo Clinic notes can help stabilize the joint after a tear.

Medication and injections

Anti-inflammatory medicine or a corticosteroid injection can ease pain and swelling, particularly when a degenerative tear sits in a knee that also has arthritis.

Meniscus repair

Arthroscopic stitching of the torn edges aims to keep the meniscus working as a shock absorber. Mayo Clinic notes that repair is sometimes possible, especially in children and younger adults.

Partial meniscectomy

When a torn flap cannot heal and keeps catching, the damaged portion is trimmed through small incisions while keeping as much healthy meniscus as possible.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo will ask how the injury happened, whether you felt a pop and whether the knee catches or locks. He presses along the joint line for tenderness, watches you walk and squat, and may perform the McMurray test, which bends, straightens and rotates the knee. X-rays help rule out arthritis or fracture because they cannot show the meniscus itself. An MRI, which Mayo Clinic calls the best imaging study for a torn meniscus, may follow.

What does recovery look like?

Recovery depends on the treatment. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, recovery after a partial meniscectomy takes about 3 to 6 weeks, and many people can put weight on the leg right away. A meniscus repair takes longer, about 3 to 6 months, because the stitched tissue needs protection while it heals. Nonsurgical recovery varies widely with the size and type of tear. Sticking with the prescribed exercises matters in every case, and your own timeline may be faster or slower.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Avoid twisting and pivoting while the knee is sore.
  • Ice for about 15 minutes at a time to manage swelling.
  • Use a compression sleeve and keep the leg elevated when you sit.
  • Over-the-counter pain relievers can help if they are safe for you.
  • Use crutches if walking makes the pain worse.

When should I get care right away?

  • The knee locks and will not straighten
  • You cannot put weight on the leg
  • Heavy swelling soon after an injury
  • Fever with redness or warmth around the knee
  • Pain, swelling or a bluish color in the calf
  • No improvement after three days of home care
