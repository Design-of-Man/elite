Kneecap Dislocation (Patellar Instability)

When your kneecap slides out of its groove, the knee can buckle, swell and look misshapen. First episodes are often treated without surgery, while repeat dislocations may need stabilization.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: patellar dislocation, patellar instability, patellar subluxation, unstable kneecap

What is a kneecap dislocation?

Your kneecap, or patella, links the thigh muscles to the shinbone and rides in a V-shaped groove at the end of the thighbone. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) explains that ligaments, muscles and the shape of the bones all work together to keep it on track. The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL), on the inner side, keeps the kneecap from moving too far sideways and can be damaged when the kneecap dislocates toward the outside.

A dislocation means the kneecap comes fully out of its groove. A subluxation is a partial slip that pops back on its own. Patellar instability is the term for a kneecap that keeps doing either. According to the AAOS, first dislocations usually happen with a pivot, twist or awkward fall, or a direct blow. Some people are more prone because of a shallow groove, knock-kneed alignment or the way the leg bones are rotated.

What are the symptoms?

  • The kneecap visibly out of place or the knee looking misshapen
  • Sudden pain and rapid swelling
  • A pop or snap at the moment of injury
  • Being unable to straighten the knee or stand on it
  • A nervous feeling that the kneecap is about to slip out
  • A kneecap that moves side to side too easily

What causes it?

  • A pivot, twist or awkward fall with the foot planted
  • A direct blow to the kneecap
  • A shallow or flat groove at the end of the thighbone
  • Knock-kneed alignment or unusual rotation of the leg bones
  • A patellar tendon that attaches farther to the outside than usual
  • A previous dislocation that stretched or tore the MPFL

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

The AAOS reports a relatively low recurrence rate after a first-time dislocation treated without surgery, so most people start with bracing and rehabilitation. MedlinePlus describes wearing a brace for about 3 weeks, followed by physical therapy to rebuild strength and motion. Surgery is usually reserved for repeat dislocations or for loose bone or cartilage fragments inside the joint. Dr. Matarazzo looks at the number of episodes, your anatomy on imaging and any cartilage damage before suggesting a stabilization procedure.

Reduction and imaging

If the kneecap stays out, it is guided back into place, often with pain medicine, and X-rays check for fractures or loose fragments.

Short period of bracing

A brace protects the healing ligament for the first few weeks before motion is gradually restored.

Physical therapy

Strengthening the thigh and hip muscles and retraining movement helps the kneecap track. The AAOS highlights stationary cycling as a useful exercise, with a gradual return to activity over 1 to 3 months.

Stabilizing brace for sport

Some people use a patellar stabilizing brace when they return to activity to reduce the sense of instability.

Patellar stabilization surgery

For recurrent instability, surgery may reinforce the soft tissue restraints, often by reconstructing the MPFL, realign the bone, or reshape the groove. The AAOS reports a redislocation rate of less than 10% after surgery.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo will ask whether this was a first dislocation or a repeat episode and how it happened. He watches you walk, checks range of motion, tests how far the kneecap moves and whether pushing it outward causes apprehension, and measures limb alignment. X-rays are standard. An MRI looks for torn ligaments and bone or cartilage fragments, and a CT scan may be ordered to study alignment and the shape of the groove.

What does recovery look like?

The NHS notes that many people can start walking again soon after treatment, but full recovery can take several months. For first-time dislocations treated without surgery, the AAOS describes a gradual return to activity over about 1 to 3 months after bracing and therapy. Recovery after stabilization surgery is longer and depends on which procedure is performed. MedlinePlus cautions that repeat dislocations can damage the knee further and raise the risk of arthritis, so finishing the full rehab program is worth the effort.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Keep the knee still and supported until it has been examined.
  • Ice the knee and keep it raised to control swelling.
  • Use over-the-counter pain relief if it is safe for you.
  • Avoid twisting and sports until strength has returned and you have been cleared.

When should I get care right away?

  • The knee has changed shape or the kneecap is visibly out of place (go to the emergency room)
  • The knee becomes very swollen very quickly after an injury (go to the emergency room)
  • You cannot stand up or move the knee
  • Pain is severe
  • Swelling and pain come back after the knee had been improving
  • The knee feels increasingly unstable
