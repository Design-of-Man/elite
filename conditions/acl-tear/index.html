ACL Tear

An ACL tear often comes with a pop and a knee that gives way. Treatment ranges from focused rehab to reconstruction, depending on how you need your knee to perform.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: anterior cruciate ligament tear, torn ACL, ACL injury, ACL rupture

What is an ACL tear?

The anterior cruciate ligament, or ACL, runs diagonally through the middle of your knee and connects the thighbone to the shinbone. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), its main job is to keep the shinbone from sliding out in front of the thighbone. That control matters most when you plant, cut, pivot or land. Tears can be partial or complete, and they often happen together with meniscus or other ligament injuries.

The AAOS estimates that more than 200,000 ACL injuries occur each year in the United States. Most happen without any direct hit to the knee, when an athlete stops suddenly, changes direction or lands awkwardly from a jump in sports such as soccer, basketball, football or volleyball. The AAOS reports that female athletes tear the ACL at two to eight times the rate of male athletes. Higher body weight, looser joints and weaker leg muscles also raise the risk.

What are the symptoms?

  • A pop heard or felt at the moment of injury
  • Swelling that appears within about 24 hours
  • The knee shifting or giving way, especially when you turn
  • Pain, particularly when you put weight on the leg
  • Loss of full range of motion
  • Tenderness along the joint line

What causes it?

  • Sudden stops or quick changes of direction
  • Landing from a jump with poor knee position
  • Direct collision during contact sports
  • Playing soccer, basketball, football or volleyball
  • Being female
  • Looser joints or reduced leg muscle strength

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

Treatment depends on how unstable the knee feels and what you want to do with it. Mayo Clinic notes that people with partial tears, no instability, or lower-demand activities may do well with rehabilitation, bracing and activity changes. Mayo Clinic generally recommends reconstruction for athletes in jumping, cutting or pivoting sports, when more than one ligament is injured, or when the knee buckles during everyday activities. Dr. Matarazzo weighs your sport or work, your age and any meniscus or cartilage damage, then explains both paths so you can decide with clear expectations.

Early swelling control

Rest, ice, compression and elevation, with crutches if needed, help the knee settle in the first days after the injury.

Physical therapy

Rehab restores knee motion and gradually rebuilds quadriceps strength, which the AAOS describes as a core part of nonsurgical care.

Bracing and activity changes

If you choose not to have surgery, a brace and stepping away from cutting and pivoting sports can reduce episodes of the knee giving way.

ACL reconstruction

The torn ligament is replaced with a graft, either a tendon from your own knee or donor tissue, usually through arthroscopic incisions. The AAOS notes reconstruction is the most common surgical approach.

Return to sport testing

Before clearance, strength, stability and movement quality are checked, since Mayo Clinic reports that up to one third of athletes have another knee injury in either knee within two years.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo will ask what you were doing when the knee gave out, whether you heard a pop and how quickly it swelled. He compares the injured knee with your healthy one, checking how far the shinbone moves forward and whether the meniscus or collateral ligaments are involved. X-rays look for avulsion fractures, where the ligament pulls a piece of bone away. An MRI shows the ACL, the menisci and the joint cartilage.

What does recovery look like?

Recovery after ACL reconstruction is measured in months. Cleveland Clinic describes a standard recovery of about 6 to 9 months, with competitive athletes often needing longer before they are cleared for sport. The early weeks focus on swelling, motion and walking normally. Strength, balance and sport-specific drills follow in stages. Clearance is usually based on how the knee performs on testing rather than the calendar alone. Nonsurgical recovery also takes dedicated rehab, and how well the knee holds up depends on the demands you place on it.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Rest the knee and use crutches if walking is painful.
  • Ice for about 20 minutes every two hours in the first days, as Mayo Clinic suggests.
  • Wrap the knee with a compression bandage and keep it elevated.
  • Avoid cutting, pivoting and jumping until the knee has been examined.

When should I get care right away?

  • You heard or felt a pop followed by swelling
  • The knee gives way when you walk or turn
  • You cannot put weight on the leg
  • The knee looks deformed or will not straighten
  • Pain, swelling, numbness or a bluish color in the calf

FAQ

ACL Tear: Common Questions

Many people can walk after an ACL tear once the first swelling settles, which is why some tears go unnoticed for a while. The AAOS explains that the ACL provides rotational stability, so problems tend to show up when you turn, pivot or step off a curb and the knee shifts. Walking on a swollen, painful knee should wait until it has been examined.

Cleveland Clinic notes that a torn ACL cannot heal on its own, but many people still manage well without surgery. Mayo Clinic notes that nonsurgical treatment with physical therapy and bracing can suit people with partial tears, those whose knees stay stable, and those who do lower-impact activities. Athletes in cutting and pivoting sports usually face more instability without reconstruction.

According to Mayo Clinic, it can take a year or more before athletes can safely return to play after ACL reconstruction. Clearance depends on strength, stability and movement control rather than a fixed date. Returning too soon raises the chance of another injury, and Mayo Clinic reports that up to one third of athletes have another knee injury within two years.

An ACL repair stitches the torn ligament back to the bone and, according to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, may be considered for certain partial tears or tears near the bone attachment. ACL reconstruction replaces the ligament with a tendon graft and is the most common surgical approach. Your surgeon chooses based on where and how the ligament tore.

Many people remember a pop and a sudden feeling that the knee shifted or buckled. Swelling usually follows within a day, and the knee may feel too stiff or painful to bend fully. Later, the main complaint is often instability, a sense that the knee cannot be trusted when you change direction, even if pain has faded.

A second opinion is reasonable for any planned surgery, including ACL reconstruction. It can help you compare graft choices, understand whether a meniscus or cartilage injury changes the plan, and confirm whether nonsurgical care might suit your goals. Bring your MRI images and report, since a second surgeon will want to review the actual pictures as well as the written read.
