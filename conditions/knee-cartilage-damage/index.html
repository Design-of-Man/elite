Knee Cartilage Damage (Chondral Defects)

A damaged patch in the smooth cartilage lining your knee can cause aching and swelling with activity. Options run from rehab and injections to cartilage restoration surgery.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: chondral defect, focal cartilage defect, articular cartilage injury, osteochondral lesion

What is knee cartilage damage?

Articular cartilage is the smooth, white tissue that covers the ends of the bones inside your knee so they glide with very little friction. Cleveland Clinic points out that this cartilage has no direct blood supply, so it usually takes longer to heal than other tissues. Mayo Clinic describes a focal defect as a pothole in otherwise normal cartilage. Even a small one can change how load passes through the joint and may lead to arthritis over time.

Focal cartilage injuries are different from the widespread thinning of osteoarthritis. They usually involve one area and often follow a knee injury. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) notes that cartilage damage is often found while a doctor is treating a related injury, such as a meniscus or ligament tear. According to the AAOS, the best candidates for restoration procedures are usually young adults with a single injury.

What are the symptoms?

  • Knee pain during or after activity, especially weight-bearing
  • Swelling that comes and goes, often after exercise
  • Stiffness or difficulty moving the knee smoothly
  • Catching, clicking or grinding as the knee bends
  • Pain with stairs, squatting or jumping

What causes it?

  • Sports injuries to the knee
  • A direct blow or fall onto the knee
  • Meniscus or ACL tears that change how the joint is loaded
  • A kneecap dislocation that chips the joint surface
  • Gradual wear over many years

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

Because cartilage repairs itself poorly, treatment aims to calm symptoms and protect the joint. Mayo Clinic notes that physical therapy and injections can reduce symptoms and may slow progression, but they do not replace lost cartilage. When a defect keeps causing pain in an otherwise healthy knee, the AAOS describes several restoration procedures. Dr. Matarazzo considers the size and location of the defect, your age, weight, leg alignment and activity goals, and whether other injuries need treatment at the same time.

Physical therapy

Strengthening the thigh and hip muscles and refining movement can reduce load on the damaged area and ease symptoms during daily activity.

Injections

Viscosupplement injections may calm pain and swelling. Mayo Clinic cautions that corticosteroid injections can interfere with the knee's ability to form repair tissue, so they are generally not advised for cartilage defects.

Orthobiologics, with realistic expectations

Some patients consider platelet-rich plasma or bone marrow concentrate injections. Evidence is still developing, and these injections do not regrow cartilage.

Marrow stimulation

Small holes are made in the bone beneath the defect so a repair tissue can form. The AAOS notes this fibrocartilage is less durable and works best for small lesions.

Osteochondral grafts

A plug of healthy cartilage and bone from a low-load area of your knee, or from a donor for larger defects, is moved into the damaged spot.

MACI

A two-stage procedure in which your own cartilage cells are grown on a collagen membrane and implanted. The AAOS describes it for defects larger than 2 centimeters.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo will ask about past injuries, earlier knee surgeries and which activities bring on pain or swelling. He checks for fluid in the joint, tenderness, alignment of the leg and the stability of the ligaments and menisci. The AAOS notes that cartilage does not show on X-ray, so X-rays are used to check alignment and look for arthritis. An MRI is usually ordered to see the cartilage surface and the bone beneath it.

What does recovery look like?

Recovery after cartilage restoration is slower than after many other knee procedures, because new tissue has to mature. The AAOS notes that patients may need crutches for several weeks and physical therapy that can include continuous passive motion, and that a full return to sports takes several months. The size and location of the defect, the procedure used and any combined surgery all change the timeline. Nonsurgical care also takes patience, and symptoms may fluctuate with activity level.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Choose lower-impact exercise such as cycling or swimming while the knee is irritable.
  • Ice the knee for up to 15 minutes after activity if it swells.
  • Keep a healthy weight to limit load on the joint.
  • Avoid deep squats and jumping until the knee has been evaluated.

When should I get care right away?

  • The knee locks or will not straighten fully
  • Swelling that returns repeatedly after activity
  • You cannot put weight on the leg
  • Fever, redness or warmth around the knee
  • Symptoms that do not improve after three days of home care
