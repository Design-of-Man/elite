Knee Arthritis

Knee arthritis can make stairs, walks and even sleep harder. Care usually starts with exercise, weight management and medication, with surgery saved for when those stop helping.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: knee osteoarthritis, degenerative joint disease of the knee, bone on bone knee

What is knee arthritis?

Your knee is where the thighbone, shinbone and kneecap meet. The ends of those bones are covered with smooth cartilage that lets them glide. In osteoarthritis, the most common form, that cartilage gradually wears away. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) describes it as a wear and tear condition seen most often in people over 50. As the cushion thins, the joint space narrows and bone spurs can form.

There are several kinds of knee arthritis. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the joint, often in both knees at once. Posttraumatic arthritis can follow a fracture, a meniscus tear or a ligament injury, sometimes years later. According to Mayo Clinic, the risk of osteoarthritis rises with age and is higher in women, in people carrying extra weight, and in people whose joints have been injured or stressed by repetitive work or sport.

What are the symptoms?

  • Aching pain that builds with activity
  • Stiffness first thing in the morning or after sitting
  • Swelling around the joint
  • Grinding, creaking or clicking when you bend the knee
  • Trouble fully bending or straightening the leg
  • A sense that the knee is weak or may buckle
  • Pain that flares with changes in the weather

What causes it?

  • Age, with osteoarthritis most common after 50
  • Being female
  • Extra body weight, which adds load to the joint
  • A past knee fracture, meniscus tear or ligament injury
  • Repeated heavy stress on the knee from work or sport
  • Inherited tendencies or joint shape present from birth
  • Autoimmune disease such as rheumatoid arthritis

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

There is no cure for arthritis, according to the AAOS, but a lot can be done to reduce pain and keep you moving. Dr. Matarazzo starts with the least invasive steps: strengthening and flexibility work, low-impact exercise, weight loss when it applies, and medication. The AAOS lists physical therapy, braces, anti-inflammatory medicines and corticosteroid injections among nonsurgical options and advises avoiding opioid pain pills. Surgery, such as partial or total knee replacement, becomes a conversation when these measures no longer give you a quality of life you can accept.

Exercise and physical therapy

A program that strengthens the thigh and hip muscles and keeps the knee flexible, often paired with low-impact activity such as cycling or swimming in place of running.

Weight management

The AAOS notes that losing weight reduces stress on the knee, so people who carry extra weight often find daily walking easier after even a modest loss.

Medication and injections

Oral or topical anti-inflammatory medicines and corticosteroid injections can calm flares. The AAOS suggests limiting steroid injections to three or four a year because frequent injections may add to joint damage.

Braces and walking aids

A brace, knee sleeve or cane can take some pressure off the worn joint during longer walks or busy days.

Orthobiologics, used with care

Some patients ask about platelet-rich plasma or similar injections. The AAOS says these show promise but clinical studies have yet to confirm their value for osteoarthritis, so they are discussed as an option with uncertain benefit, and they do not rebuild cartilage.

Knee replacement or the MISHA Knee System

When arthritis is advanced, partial or total knee replacement, including MAKO robotic-arm assisted surgery, resurfaces the worn joint. The MISHA implantable shock absorber may be discussed for select patients with arthritis on the inner side of the knee whose pain persists despite other care and who are not candidates for, or not ready for, total knee replacement.

What happens at the first visit?

Dr. Matarazzo will ask where the pain sits, what makes it worse, how long morning stiffness lasts and what you have already tried. He checks swelling, tenderness, range of motion, stability and the way you walk. X-rays are the usual first image because they show joint space narrowing, bone changes and spurs. Blood tests may be ordered if rheumatoid arthritis is possible, and MRI only when another injury is suspected.

What does recovery look like?

Arthritis care is ongoing, so recovery means finding a routine that keeps symptoms manageable. Exercise programs work best with steady effort over time, and flares can still come and go even when you are doing everything right. After surgery, timelines depend on the procedure. A partial knee replacement and a total knee replacement each have their own recovery, and your age, strength and general health all play a part. The AAOS is clear that no treatment cures arthritis, so the realistic goal is less pain and better function.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Swap high-impact exercise for cycling, swimming or walking on flat ground.
  • Ice the knee for up to 15 minutes after activity if it swells.
  • Over-the-counter pain relievers can help, but ask your doctor first if you have heart disease, heart failure or kidney disease, since the AAOS cautions against NSAIDs in those cases.
  • Wear cushioned, supportive shoes.
  • Keep moving a little each day instead of resting the knee completely.

When should I get care right away?

  • Fever, redness or warmth around the knee, or a lot of swelling
  • You cannot put weight on the leg
  • Severe pain even when you are not standing on it
  • The knee locks, buckles or will not straighten
  • Pain, swelling, numbness or a bluish color in the calf
  • Symptoms that do not improve after three days of home care

FAQ

Knee Arthritis: Common Questions

No current treatment reverses arthritis or regrows worn cartilage, and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons states plainly that arthritis has no cure. Treatment can still lower pain and improve function. Strength training, weight loss when it applies, activity changes, medication and injections help many people stay active for years, and joint replacement is an option when the damage becomes severe.

Low-impact exercise that builds strength without pounding the joint is usually easiest on an arthritic knee. The AAOS suggests activities such as swimming and cycling in place of jogging or high-impact sports. Strengthening the thigh and hip muscles helps the knee handle load. A physical therapist can set a starting level so you build up gradually without triggering a flare.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons recommends limiting corticosteroid injections to about three or four per year. A steroid shot can calm a painful arthritis flare for a while, but frequent injections may increase damage to the joint over time. Your surgeon will usually look at how much relief earlier injections gave before suggesting another one.

Platelet-rich plasma is an injection made from a concentrate of your own blood. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says PRP and stem cell injections show promise, but clinical studies have yet to confirm their value in treating osteoarthritis. These injections do not regrow cartilage. If you are curious, ask your surgeon how PRP would fit alongside exercise and weight management.

Knee replacement is usually considered when arthritis limits daily life, such as walking, sleeping or climbing stairs, and nonsurgical care like exercise, medication and injections no longer gives enough relief. X-rays showing advanced joint damage support the decision. The timing is a personal choice, and an orthopedic surgeon can review whether a partial or total replacement suits your knee.

For most people with knee arthritis, regular walking on flat ground is a reasonable way to stay active, and activity supports muscle strength and a healthy weight. Pace yourself, wear cushioned shoes and skip downhill running, which MedlinePlus suggests avoiding to protect the knees. If walking causes sharp pain or swelling that lingers, have the knee examined.
