Shoulder Impingement and Bursitis

Pain on the outside of your shoulder when you reach up or lie on that side often comes from impingement or bursitis, and treatment usually starts without surgery.

Schedule a Consultation 561-202-8886

Also called: subacromial impingement, subacromial pain syndrome, rotator cuff tendinitis, shoulder bursitis, swimmer's shoulder, pitcher's shoulder

About Shoulder Impingement and Bursitis

Your rotator cuff tendons pass through a narrow space beneath the acromion, the bony roof at the top of your shoulder. A small fluid-filled sac called the bursa sits between them so the tendons can glide. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, this space gets smaller when you raise your arm, and the tendons and bursa can be pinched. Irritated tendons are called tendinitis, an inflamed bursa is called bursitis, and the two often occur together.

MedlinePlus lists rotator cuff tendinitis and bursitis as the most common causes of shoulder pain. The problem shows up in young athletes who swim, pitch, play tennis or play volleyball, and in people who paint, build or lift overhead for hours. Holding the arm in one position for long stretches (computer work, hairstyling), sleeping on the same arm every night, years of poor posture and aging can all contribute.

What are the symptoms?

  • Pain in the front of the shoulder that can spread to the side of the arm
  • Pain when you lift the arm out to the side or overhead, or lower it from a raised position
  • Mild pain at rest that becomes more noticeable over time
  • Night pain, especially lying on the affected side
  • Trouble reaching behind your back
  • Loss of strength and motion as symptoms advance

What causes it?

  • Repetitive overhead sports such as swimming, baseball, tennis and volleyball
  • Overhead or repetitive lifting at work, as in painting and construction
  • Holding the arm in one position for long periods, such as computer work or hairstyling
  • Sleeping on the same shoulder every night
  • Poor posture over many years
  • Aging and bone spurs under the acromion

How is it treated, with and without surgery?

Impingement and bursitis are usually treated without an operation. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists rest, anti-inflammatory medicine, physical therapy and steroid injections into the bursa, and notes that full recovery can take several weeks to months. Surgery plays a smaller role than it once did. A 2018 placebo-controlled trial in The Lancet (CSAW) found that arthroscopic subacromial decompression offered no extra benefit over arthroscopy alone, so a thorough course of rehab comes first in Dr. Matarazzo's approach.

Relative rest and activity changes

Cutting back overhead reaching, throwing or lifting while symptoms are active lets the tendons and bursa settle, without fully immobilizing the arm.

Physical therapy

Stretching improves shoulder motion, and strengthening the rotator cuff and shoulder blade muscles helps the tendons move through the narrow space with less irritation.

Anti-inflammatory medicine and ice

Ibuprofen or naproxen, plus ice applied for about 20 minutes at a time, can reduce pain and swelling when they are safe for you.

Subacromial steroid injection

A corticosteroid placed into the bursa can quiet inflammation enough to make exercise tolerable.

Arthroscopic surgery for persistent cases

If pain continues after a sustained course of nonsurgical care, arthroscopy can remove inflamed bursa and, in selected cases, bone spurs. Given the CSAW findings, this is reserved for carefully chosen patients.

What happens at the first visit?

Expect questions about your sport or job, which motions hurt, night pain and how long it has lasted. Dr. Matarazzo checks range of motion and arm strength, places the arm in positions that typically reproduce impingement pain, and screens your neck, since pain traveling past the elbow can come from a pinched nerve. X-rays are often normal but may show bone spurs. MRI or ultrasound is used if a rotator cuff tear is suspected.

What does recovery look like?

Recovery from impingement and bursitis is usually measured in weeks to months. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says nonsurgical treatment can take several weeks to months for complete recovery, and NHS guidance notes shoulder pain can take 6 months or longer to settle. According to MedlinePlus, some people need to cut back on a particular sport to stay comfortable. If arthroscopic surgery is done, AAOS says complete pain relief usually takes 2 to 4 months and may take up to a year.

What can I do in the meantime?

  • Scale back the overhead motion that sets off your pain, while still moving the arm through comfortable ranges.
  • Wrap an ice pack in a thin towel and apply it for up to 20 minutes, up to 3 times a day.
  • Set up your desk so you are not reaching forward or up for long periods.
  • Try sleeping on your back or your other side instead of on the sore shoulder.

When should I get care right away?

  • Sudden loss of strength, or being unable to lift the arm, which can signal a rotator cuff tear
  • Pain that spreads past the elbow into the hand, or pins and needles that will not go away
  • Fever, redness or swelling along with shoulder pain
  • Symptoms that have not eased after 2 to 4 weeks of home care
  • Call 911 for sudden pressure or crushing pain in the shoulder, especially if it runs from the chest to the left jaw, arm or neck, or comes with shortness of breath, dizziness or sweating

FAQ

Shoulder Impingement and Bursitis: Common Questions

They are closely related. Impingement describes the pinching of the rotator cuff tendons and bursa under the acromion when you lift your arm. Bursitis is inflammation of that bursa, and tendinitis is irritation of the tendons themselves. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons discusses them together because they often occur at the same time and respond to the same first-line treatments.

Mild cases often improve once you reduce the activity that triggers them. MedlinePlus notes that rest, exercise and self-care often improve or relieve rotator cuff tendinitis, though this may take weeks or months. If pain keeps returning or is getting worse at night, an exam can check for a rotator cuff tear, which can feel similar early on and is managed differently.

Most people do not. Nonsurgical care is the standard first approach. A randomized trial published in The Lancet in 2018 (CSAW) compared subacromial decompression with arthroscopy alone and found no added benefit from removing bone, and the gain over no surgery was not considered clinically important. Surgery may still be discussed when pain persists after thorough rehab or when imaging shows another problem, such as a tear.

A corticosteroid injection into the subacromial bursa is one of the treatments the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists for impingement and bursitis. It can calm inflammation and pain, which often makes it easier to do the strengthening exercises that address the underlying problem. The effect wears off over time, so the injection works best as part of a rehab plan.

Programs usually combine stretching to loosen a tight shoulder with strengthening of the rotator cuff and the muscles that move the shoulder blade. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists physical therapy built around these two goals. NHS guidance suggests keeping up shoulder exercises for 6 to 8 weeks to help stop pain returning, even after symptoms ease. Avoid heavy overhead lifting while the shoulder is irritated.

Night pain is common with impingement and bursitis. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that the pain is typically at its worst at night, and lying on the affected shoulder can press on the irritated tendons and bursa. Sleeping on your back or other side may help. Night pain that regularly wakes you, especially with weakness, is worth having checked.
